\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}
We use three named measurement configurations. \emph{Isolated} denotes controlled engine
characterization on a pinned core, \emph{Replay} denotes real-data replay under normal CFS
scheduling, and \emph{Bare-metal} denotes evaluation on the AArch64 \texttt{no\_std} target.
All synthetic-fixture timing follows the Isolated configuration of our measurement policy. The
host uses \envCpu{} and \envKernel{}. The benchmark core is
isolated with \texttt{isolcpus} and \texttt{nohz\_full}; IRQ affinities are moved, with
three immovable IRQs remaining. Its SMT sibling is offline, and the \envGovernor{}
governor pins its frequency at \SI{3.2}{GHz}. We compile C++ with \envCppCompiler{} using
\texttt{CMAKE\_BUILD\_TYPE=Release} and \envCppReleaseFlags. Rust uses
\envRustCompiler{} with Cargo release defaults \texttt{opt-level=3},
\texttt{lto=false}, and \texttt{codegen-units=16}, plus the workspace override
\texttt{overflow-checks=true}. No target CPU is explicitly selected, and the manifests
record no experiment-specific \texttt{RUSTFLAGS}.

The campaign comprises \envSessions{} sessions from distinct boots, with randomized
fixture and engine order. For each fixture and engine, every session records
\envSamplesPerSession{} measured alignments after \envWarmup{} warmups, yielding
\envPooledSamples{} pooled samples per fixture and engine. We pool these samples across
sessions for the warm series; Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-interference} reports matched-size cold
and co-runner series. A \texttt{steady\_clock} measures only the align loop, after the map
and kd-tree have been built once per engine and fixture. A fixed-work calibration guard
rejects a cell whose sustained speed shifts by more than 10\%; it fired once, and the
affected cell was excluded and remeasured. Engines were measured at
autoware\_core commit \texttt{\envCommit}. The released data files embed per-run
manifests containing the environment, compiler flags, commits, and fixture and binary hashes.

\subsection{Search outcomes and ablation limits}
\label{sec:eval-search}
The domain-informed seed represented by the hand-built \emph{dense-neighbors} construction
saturates the Rust envelope's analytic maximum for the common kernel-evaluation term:
$\sumnbr = P \cdot \maxnn \cdot (N_{\mathrm{iter}}{+}1) = 2000 \cdot 64 \cdot 31 =
\num{3968000}$. This construction is therefore kernel-count-optimal under the configured
$P_{\max}$, \maxnn, and $I_{\max}$. The climb then increases the Rust-specific traversal
term \kdnodes{} by a further
\searchKdGrowthPct\%, from \oneoffKdGenZeroM\,M to \searchZeroKdM\,M, while increasing
the map extent from 6 to 9 blocks. The generation-0 value comes from the recorded search
log and the final value from the frozen fixture's counters.
Thus \emph{search-00} is the Rust-search champion transferred to both engines. Among the
primary transferred fixtures in Table~\ref{tab:tails}, it is slower for both engines at
every reported percentile. This ordering applies only to the evaluated fixture set. The
frontier ablation below refines the Rust result, and Table~\ref{tab:counters} lists the
frozen set.

\input{tables/counters}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports a limited search ablation. It supports three scoped
observations rather than a ranking of search strategies. First, the analytic construction,
not the climb, reaches the kernel-count axis: across \ablationSeeds{} seeds the
domain-informed seed genome attains the \sumnbr{} maximum at evaluation~1. Second, the
climb contributes the ${+}$\searchKdGrowthPct\% increase in the Rust traversal term
\kdnodes{}. Third, the fixed-seed counter search is deterministic, whereas the two recorded
wall-clock-fitness runs with one shared seed return different champions:
\ablationTimeKdA{} versus \ablationTimeKdB{} \kdnodes{}, a difference of only
\ablationTimeKdDeltaPct\%. This pair illustrates sensitivity to timing noise but does not
estimate its frequency.

The ablation has four informed-seed hill runs, three random-genome runs, and two
wall-clock-fitness runs. Budget-matched random sampling uses \ablationBudget{} evaluations
and spans \ablationRandomWorstPct--\ablationRandomBestPct\% of the \sumnbr{} maximum.
Because its initial distribution differs from the hill runs, the comparison confounds seed
quality with search strategy and supports no general claim that hill-climbing or
counter-guided search outperforms the alternatives.

The base diagnostics also
show identical \texttt{iteration\_num} per engine pair, final poses agreeing to at most
\certMaxTransDelta\,\si{m} of translation, and both convergence scores bit-identical on

The largest score delta is \certMaxScoreDelta{} on \emph{subnormal}, or one \texttt{f32} ULP.
The primary timing comparison therefore uses trace-conformant inputs.
Table~\ref{tab:tails} reports the unified ten-fixture campaign, including the two archived

larger maximum on \tailsRustSlowerCount{}, with per-fixture ratios spanning
\tailsRatioMin--\tailsRatioMax. The engine-specific fixture maxima are
\SI{\combinedFixtureMaxCpp}{ms} for C++ and \SI{\combinedFixtureMaxRust}{ms} for Rust.

The Pareto rows use the same $3{\times}1000$ protocol as every other fixture. The Rust
record occurs on \emph{pareto-01}; its p50 also exceeds the \emph{search-00} anchor, so the

The \emph{subnormal} fixture's p50 ratio of $\subnormalMedianRatio\times$ motivated a
controlled check. Regenerating the same fixture with the source shell moved from
\SI{1.99}{m} to \SI{1.69}{m}, which moves every kernel $\exp$ result out of the subnormal
band with all else equal, did \emph{not} reduce the time under the same Isolated protocol.
C++ p50 rises from
\SI{\oneoffShellSubCpp}{ms} to \SI{\oneoffShellCtrlCpp}{ms}, and Rust p50 rises from
\oneoffShellSubRust{} to \oneoffShellCtrlRust. On this microarchitecture, subnormal
operands are therefore \emph{not} the mechanism on this fixture. A microbenchmark confirms
that subnormal-result \texttt{exp} costs only
$\oneoffExpSubGlibc\times$/$\oneoffExpSubLibm\times$ per call for glibc/\texttt{libm},
negligible at this fixture's $\bar{K}=\subnormalKbar$.

The remaining difference is consistent with \emph{per-point constant overhead} exposed by
a neighbor-sparse shape. With almost no kernel work, C++ spends
${\approx}\subnormalPtPassCppUs$\,\si{\micro\second} per point per pass against Rust's
$\subnormalPtPassRustUs$, an ${\approx}\ptPassGapUs$\,\si{\micro\second} gap that reappears
as the same constant on \emph{cache-hostile}, where
$\bar{K}=\cacheHostileKbar$. The measured allocator fast path costs
\SI{\oneoffMallocPairNs}{ns} per malloc/free pair across approximately
\subnormalAllocPairs{} pairs and accounts for only
${\sim}\subnormalAllocSharePct\%$. The remainder is the per-query search machinery
around PCL/FLANN, including call indirection and result-container handling, which the Rust
engine's direct, allocation-free kd query avoids. This refines the allocation analysis of
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-alloc}: heap churn is inexpensive \emph{on average}, and its significance is
that it is a known input-proportional latency path absent from the Rust kernel. Removing it
does not bound the remaining platform effects; the mean-cost gap on sparse inputs is
consistent with search-machinery overhead. Hardware performance counters are unavailable
in the measurement container, so the final split is inferred from microbenchmarks rather
than attributed with hardware counters.
Section~\ref{sec:threats} records this limitation.

\subsection{Per-input work-trace conformance}
\label{sec:eval-tracecert}
\input{tables/tracecert}

The work-trace conformance check of Sec.~\ref{sec:meth-transfer} was applied to every
frozen fixture and to all \realFrames{} captured real frames. The traced analysis build of
the C++ engine uses an instrumented copy of the engine sources plus a counting FLANN
distance functor; the shipped sources remain byte-identical. It emits a per-pass trace that
the replay compares leg by leg against the Rust engine's mirrored trace. Every quantity is
a deterministic function of the input, so the runs need no controlled timing environment,
and the traced binaries are never used for timing.

Table~\ref{tab:tracecert} shows the fixture leg. The \emph{shape} fields comprise pass
count, per-pass point and neighbor counts, and SHA-256 digests of each point's leaf IDs
after canonical sorting. Shape agrees on all \traceFixtures{} inputs, the line-search loop
is never entered, and the measured C++ pass count equals $N_{\mathrm{iter}}{+}1$ on every
input. This directly establishes equality of the recorded work topology, rather than
inferring it from iterations or output proximity. The independent payload digest, which
also records leaf means and regularized inverse covariances, agrees on
\tracePayloadExactFixtures{} of \traceFixtures{} fixtures. The payload result demonstrates
that shape agreement does not establish equality of all kernel operands.

The C++ neighbor count is the complete result of an uncapped radius search, not a
post-search prefix, whereas the Rust count is the at-most-64 list returned by its capped
search. Shape agreement therefore confirms the bound along the C++ query trajectory.
It does not confirm the counterfactual uncapped count at each Rust query coordinate, which
may differ slightly from its C++ counterpart; that distinction prevents the trace result
from being interpreted as membership in $D$.

The per-pass score agrees to \traceUlpConvMax{} f64 ULP or better on the
well-conditioned fixtures and is bit-identical on \emph{cache-hostile}, but drifts to
$\traceUlpWorstTex$ ULP on the most ill-conditioned geometries. PCL/FLANN returns each
point's neighbors sorted by distance while the port's kd walk returns them in visit order,
so the engines may accumulate the same shape in different orders. This explains why exact
floating-point sums are not required by $E_{\mathrm{shape}}$. SHA-256 makes accidental
digest equality computationally unlikely but does not turn this per-input check into a
formal proof.

The real-data leg separates iteration, output, shape, and payload agreement. Of the
\realBaseOnMap{} on-map frames in $E_{\mathrm{base}}$, \realShapeOnMap{} also belong to
$E_{\mathrm{shape}}$, while \traceRealWorkGapFrames{} are base-only. In those five
frames, the point--neighbor evaluation counts differ by a median
$|\Delta\sumnbr|$ of \traceRealWorkGapNbrMed{} and a maximum of
\traceRealWorkGapNbrMax, at most \traceRealWorkGapRelMaxPct\% of the frame's kernel
evaluations. Across all on-map frames, \realShapeStructuralOnMap{} have matching shape
before applying the base criterion, but only \tracePayloadExactOnMap{} have an exact
payload digest. Thus even a matching canonical leaf-ID sequence usually does not imply
bit-identical leaf parameters.

The exhaustive on-map partition is \realShapeOnMap{} trace-conformant,
\realBaseOnly{} base-only, and \realBaseDivergent{} outside $E_{\mathrm{base}}$.
Among the last group, \realOutputDivergent{} retain equal iteration counts but exceed at
least one pose or score threshold, whereas \realIterationDivergent{} take different
iteration paths. The iteration-divergent frames fork between passes
\traceRealDivFirstMin{} and \traceRealDivFirstMax{}, with median
\traceRealDivFirstMed{}. Their iteration counts differ by up to
$\pm\traceRealDivIterDeltaMax$, and their final poses differ by up to
\SI{\traceRealDivergentTransDeltaMax}{m}. Fixture timing comparisons are restricted to
the trace-conformant fixtures; real-data strict comparisons are restricted to
$E_{\mathrm{shape}}$.

\subsection{Heap allocations per align}
\label{sec:eval-alloc}
The interposer counts decompose as ${\approx}\allocPerPtPass$ allocations per point per
derivative pass on every fixture. For example,
$\allocWorstPerAlign{} / (31 \cdot 2000) = \allocPerPtPassExact$. This locates the
source in the per-point inner loop: a fresh neighbor
vector constructed inside the parallel loop body, two fresh index/distance vectors per
radius-search call, and approximately eight per-query result containers per point inside PCL/FLANN.
These allocations use the allocator's thread-cache fast path and have low mean cost, but
each is a
potential lock, page fault, or fragmentation stall inside the innermost loop. The Rust
engine hoists all three allocation sites into a pre-reserved workspace and measures zero in
the same harness, consistent with the counting-allocator-verified allocation freedom shown
in Table~\ref{tab:alloc}.

Beyond the hazard reading, these counts provide the \emph{independent C++-side work check}
defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:meth-transfer}. The numerator comes from the
untouched production binary via \texttt{LD\_PRELOAD}, while the denominator
$P \cdot N_{\mathrm{pass}}$ comes from the Rust counters. The ratio is
\allocCertMin--\allocCertMax{} on every fixture whose radius queries return neighbors.
The \allocCertSpreadPct\% spread remains small across geometries whose cost differs by two
orders of magnitude. A single extra C++ derivative pass, which would shift the count by
about 3\% at $N_{\mathrm{pass}} = 31$, would exceed the observed spread. The
one deviation, \emph{subnormal} at \allocCertSparse{}, is itself structural: its
mostly-empty radius searches skip the per-result containers.

Table~\ref{tab:interference} reports two further Isolated series that probe the
warm-series results. The \emph{cold} series uses between-sample software cache eviction;
the largest positive median change is $+\coldWorstPct\%$ across the whole fixture set. The

the engine-configuration stress run, consistent with its kd-dominated cost structure.

\subsection{Unit-cost regression}
\label{sec:eval-regression}
Regression is diagnostic, not unit-cost identification. Across the fixtures and $P$-sweep
points, the worst regressor correlation is \regCorr, bootstrap intervals are wide, and the
adopted Rust model has a \regLooMaxRust\% worst leave-one-out error. The current counter
vector therefore does not support general latency prediction. Moreover, each engine uses a
different traversal counter. We use the counters to target structural work and base the
implementation comparison on direct fixture maxima in Table~\ref{tab:tails}, not
coefficient attribution. The models, coefficients, and intervals appear in
Table~\ref{tab:regression} of Appendix~\ref{app:regression}.

\subsection{Scaling with the input size $P$}
\label{sec:eval-psweep}
We regenerate the Rust-search geometry at six values
$P\in[\psweepPmin{},\psweepPmax{}]$, increasing by a factor of two at each step. In this
controlled sweep, $P$ is the only varied multiplicative term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp}; it
scales the per-iteration sum while $N_{\mathrm{iter}}$, $K$, and $T_{\mathrm{solve}}$
remain fixed. This construction predicts an \emph{affine} relation
$T_{\mathrm{obs,max}}(P) =
\mathrm{slope}\cdot P + \mathrm{const}$. This is a fit to observed maxima, not a hard bound.
The counters verify that per-point work is exactly invariant: $N_{\mathrm{iter}} = 30$ and
$\sumnbr = P \cdot 64 \cdot 31$ at every $P$. Within this constructed family, the modeled work therefore varies only through $P$; platform noise remains in the timing samples.
Table~\ref{tab:psweep} and Fig.~\ref{fig:psweep} show the result: an affine line closely fits
the \emph{max} series, with $R^2 = \psweepRsqCpp{}$ for C++ and
$\psweepRsqRust{}$ for Rust. The largest absolute residual is
\SI{\psweepResidualCpp}{ms} for C++ at $P=\psweepResidualPCpp{}$ and
\SI{\psweepResidualRust}{ms} for Rust at $P=\psweepResidualPRust{}$; no cache knee is
visible over the tested range. The fitted
observed-maximum slope is
\SI{\psweepSlopeCpp}{\micro\second} for C++ versus
\SI{\psweepSlopeRust}{\micro\second} for Rust
per source point, consistent with the unit costs of
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-regression}: approximately 64 kernel evaluations across 31 passes per
point, plus the per-point and traversal terms.

Inverting the fitted observed-maximum line against the 10\,Hz, \SI{100}{ms} budget gives an
\emph{empirical budget crossover} at $P \approx \psweepCrossRust{}$ for Rust and
\psweepCrossCpp{} for C++ under the transferred Rust-search geometry on this host. The Rust
value interpolates within the measured range, whereas the C++ value is a short extrapolation
below its lower endpoint. These point estimates are neither hard admission limits nor WCET
bounds. Table~\ref{tab:psweep-fit} reports the slope and intercept intervals and residuals;
the same protocol is required to obtain a platform-specific crossover on deployment
hardware.

\subsection{Two-tier input-domain analysis}
\label{sec:eval-twotier}
The adversarial fixtures are constructed stress witnesses for the engine API over the
bounded-neighbor domain $D$; they are not upper bounds on that domain.
This is the bounded-neighbor engine-input domain defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:meth-transfer}. It is the
relevant contract for a \texttt{no\_std} kernel deployment in which inputs cross a trust
boundary without production preprocessing validation. When the production
preprocessing contracts of Sec.~\ref{sec:impl-legal} hold, the reachable input space
shrinks. The input geometry of \emph{geom-stress} satisfies those contracts, but its run
uses non-shipped $\epsilon=10^{-10}$ to force 30 iterations. Its observed maximum is
\SI{\legalWorstMaxRust}{ms} for Rust and \SI{\legalWorstMaxCpp}{ms} for C++. These are
measured factors of \legalWorstRatioRust$\times$ and \legalWorstRatioCpp$\times$ below
the transferred Rust-search fixture in Table~\ref{tab:tails}. These are measured
witness-to-witness gaps under stress configurations, not shipped-configuration performance
ratios.

Two caveats scope this factor. It is a \emph{fixture-to-fixture measurement}, not a proven
tightening: \emph{geom-stress} realizes the voxel-center heuristic $K = 8$ per point, while
the production-input domain's analytic ceiling is $K \le 27$ under the assumptions of
Sec.~\ref{sec:implementation}. The contracts alone reduce the parametric neighbor factor
from 64 to 27, a $64/27 \approx 2.4\times$ contraction; a contract-conforming input between
our witness and that ceiling may exist.
The bound is also conditional on the contracts because the engine does not check them.
Table~\ref{tab:preconditions} lists these assumptions.

The mechanism is the $K$ ceiling: disjoint voxel-aligned map tiles cap leaf multiplicity at
1, capping $K$ at 27 instead of 64. This witness realizes
$\bar{K} = \legalWorstKbar$, and the production random-downsample cap fixes
$P \le 1500$. The cost structure also shifts: kernel evaluations drop
$\twoTierKernelDrop\times$, with $\sumnbr$ falling
from $\searchZeroSumNbrM\,\mathrm{M}$ to $\legalWorstSumNbrK\,\mathrm{k}$, but time
drops less, because kd-tree traversal, unaffected by the $K$ ceiling, becomes the dominant
term. The counter reduction illustrates the contraction in modeled work, whereas the timing
gap also reflects fixture geometry and configuration and cannot be attributed solely to
input-contract enforcement. Independently, the shipped-configuration search of
Sec.~\ref{sec:impl-legal} reaches the full 30-iteration cap on \emph{shipped-osc}. It costs
\SI{\legalOscMaxRust}{ms} in Rust and \SI{\legalOscMaxCpp}{ms} in C++, with
$\bar{K} = \legalOscKbar$. This establishes iteration-cap reachability under shipped
parameters, not a worst-case timing input. At the same shipped $\epsilon$, the
\emph{geom-stress} input converges in \oneoffLegalWorstProdEpsIters{} iterations.

\subsection{Real-data replay: cropped-map and degraded-prior stress}
\label{sec:eval-realdata}
To relate the two tiers to real inputs, we used an environment-gated hook at the engine
entry points to capture each NDT input: the map tile, per-align guess, and source cloud. The
capture contains \realFrames{} aligns collected after production preprocessing during a
57-minute urban drive in İstanbul. Both engines replayed the resulting buffers offline with
cost counters enabled. The harness could not reproduce the production localization filter's
prior quality, so the frozen guess track uses NDT odometry with constant-velocity
extrapolation from a global-navigation-satellite-system (GNSS) seed. This track represents
degraded-prior operation, including initialization and filter dropouts, rather than nominal
cruising; the converged node-level baseline requires three iterations per frame.

The offline benchmark map is cropped, and the reconstructed trajectory exits it after frame
\realOnMapSeqMax{}. Only \realOnMap{} aligns, distributed across \realSegments{} short
segments in this prefix and covering approximately 2\% of the drive, perform meaningful
matching work. We classify a frame as \emph{on-map} when at least one radius search returns
a neighbor, so $\sumnbr>0$. Every search is empty on the remaining \realOffMap{}
\emph{off-map} frames, making those aligns near-no-ops with a median of
\realOffMapIterMed{} iterations and a C++ p50 of approximately
\SI{\realOffMapCppMed}{ms}. We therefore treat the experiment as a \emph{cropped-map,
degraded-prior stress replay}, useful for exposing map-boundary numerical branching,
stressing initialization and dropout priors, and identifying port-fidelity failures. It is
not a representative nominal-localization workload over the full recording.

Table~\ref{tab:realdata} separates these populations. Its first panel reports $P$, maximum
$K$, and the partition by base agreement and trace conformance over all frames. The second reports timing over
all \realOnMap{} on-map frames as a system-level observation and over the
\realShapeOnMap{}-frame $E_{\mathrm{shape}}$ subset as the strict trace-conformant
implementation comparison. The all-frame counts provide capture context, whereas the
near-no-op off-map majority does not characterize localization fidelity.

The input counters are consistent with the preprocessing contracts: $P\le\realPMax=1500$
on all \realFrames{} frames. The per-point neighbor count reaches at most
$\realKmaxMax$, which is within the geometric bound of 27 but one above the voxel-center
heuristic of 8. Real centroids can deviate from voxel centers and bring a ninth leaf into
range; 27, rather than 8, is the applicable bound. Under degraded priors, the iteration cap
is reached on \realIterCapPct\% of on-map frames, showing that the iteration cap is
reachable on captured production-preprocessed geometry under this degraded-prior replay.

The timing rows report the \emph{Replay} configuration. They use
normal CFS scheduling, no pinning, one align per frame in production order, and a pinned
\SI{3.2}{GHz} reference clock on all cores. The observed-maximum frame costs
\SI{\realCppMax}{ms} in C++ and \SI{\realRustMax}{ms} in Rust. Under this track, the
10\,Hz deadline is no longer a marginal worst case: \realOverrunsCpp{} C++
aligns exceed the \SI{100}{ms} budget. They represent
\realOverrunsCppOnMapPct\% of the on-map frames, whose C++ median is
\SI{\realCppMedOnMap}{ms}. Rust has no overruns. The Rust maximum sits
$\realMarginDeploy\times$ below the \emph{geom-stress} run and
${\sim}\realMarginEngine\times$ below \emph{search-00}. These reference maxima are
Isolated values. For Rust, the bridge experiment justifies the comparison with
Replay-to-Isolated ratios of
$\bridgeRustInflMin$--$\bridgeRustInflMax\times$, as reported in
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-bridge}.
The comparison is specific to the recorded reference clock; a production ECU may have
different governor and boost behavior. The replay also excludes co-resident Autoware
workloads to isolate align-kernel timing and preserve cross-engine comparability. It
therefore does not establish full-stack deadline compliance. The counter rows are
environment-independent.

The conformance levels differ materially. Over all frames,
$|E_{\mathrm{base}}|=\realBaseAll{}$ and
$|E_{\mathrm{shape}}|=\realShapeAll{}$. The exhaustive on-map partition is
\realShapeOnMap{} trace-conformant frames, \realBaseOnly{} base-only frames, and
\realBaseDivergent{} frames outside $E_{\mathrm{base}}$. Thus
\realShapeOnMapPct\% of meaningful matches support the strict work-trace comparison;
\realNotShapeOnMap{} frames, or \realNotShapeOnMapPct\%, do not. The last group contains
\realOutputDivergent{} frames with equal iteration counts but excessive pose or score
differences and \realIterationDivergent{} frames with different iteration counts.
% DATA-CHECKED(2026-07-15): all frames outside E_base are on-map; gen_tables.py enforces this.
The initial divergences exposed two port-fidelity gaps that translation-only synthetic
fixtures could not reveal. Fixing both reduced the iteration-count divergence to
\realIterationDivergent{} frames, or \realOnMapDivPct\% of on-map frames and
\realDivPctAll\% of all captured frames. Section~\ref{sec:threats} characterizes both
the iteration and equal-iteration output residuals. The traced replay of
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-tracecert} records their per-engine outputs and work shape.
The \realIterationDivergent{} divergences cluster in \realDivSegments{} of
\realSegments{} on-map segments but do not exhibit unusually large work counts:
$\bar{K}$ spans \realDivKbarMin{}--\realDivKbarMax{}, and the iteration count spans
\realDivIterMin--\realDivIterMax{}.
At the maximum-delta frame Rust stops after 17 iterations while C++ reaches 30, amplifying
a small numerical perturbation to \SI{\traceRealDivergentTransDeltaMax}{m}. Such sensitivity
is not language-specific: C++ library, compiler, contraction, or ISA changes can also perturb
accumulation, eigensolution, or convergence. The open-loop replay did not measure that
C++-to-C++ variability.

The pose deltas in Table~\ref{tab:realdata} measure substitutability rather than localization
error because only one engine runs in production and the replay has no ground truth.

Stack-level replay extends the comparison to the NDT--EKF interface, including rotation,
quality-gate, innovation, and feedback behavior. We replayed the same 57-minute cloud,

Table~\ref{tab:realdata}, the stack used the deployed four-worker configuration: four OpenMP
threads for C++ and a four-worker Rayon pool for Rust. The workers remained under CFS without
per-worker pinning, so this is a throughput observation rather than multi-core WCET evidence.
Each run consumed 34,375 clouds at \SI{10.00}{Hz}. C++ NDT maxima were
\SI{45.7}{ms}--\SI{46.8}{ms} and Rust maxima were
\SI{15.0}{ms}--\SI{20.7}{ms}; no alignment exceeded \SI{100}{ms}. The different parallelism and

from 14,779 to 21,334 for C++ and 18,096 to 30,711 for Rust because feedback trajectories
left the available map region. C++ therefore had per-align compute headroom for \SI{10}{Hz}; missing

At the open-loop maximum-delta frame, all runs passed the NDT quality and EKF gates, and the

difference. Whole-drive
repeatability was insufficient for attribution: the C++/Rust repeats disagreed on

\subsection{Bridge experiment: Isolated and Replay configurations}
\label{sec:eval-bridge}
Our measurement policy links the two configurations through a mandatory bridge experiment.
The same five frozen inputs are replayed under both configurations: the transferred
Rust-search fixture, \emph{geom-stress}, \emph{shipped-osc}, and the slowest and

use the same \SI{3.2}{GHz} reference clock and harness, differing only in the environment.
Table~\ref{tab:bridge} reports medians, maxima, and the descriptive
interference-inflation ratio, defined as the Replay maximum divided by the Isolated maximum.
The synthetic legs are
matched-$n$, using a pooled three-session $n{=}3000$ campaign in both configurations, so their
max-vs-max ratios are same-$n$; the real-frame legs are $n{=}100$ in both configurations.

For the \textbf{Rust} engine, the ratio is
$\bridgeRustInflMin$--$\bridgeRustInflMax\times$ on every input: the controlled maxima

$\bridgeCppInflMin$--$\bridgeCppInflMax\times$. The controlled environment is not
engine-neutral. A same-boot control provides partial localization of the difference. Pinned
to the isolated core, the C++
\emph{shipped-osc} align costs \SI{194.9}{ms}, compared with \SI{165.8}{ms} on a
non-isolated core in the same boot under the same affinity policy; Rust is unchanged in the
same binary. The Isolated
configuration is thus
\emph{associated} with a \SI{\bridgeCppTaxMinMs}{}--\SI{\bridgeCppTaxMaxMs}{ms} higher C++

and the per-core \texttt{isolcpus}, \texttt{nohz\_full}, and \texttt{rcu\_nocbs} flags

consistent with a contribution from allocation or per-query machinery, but the available
controls do not isolate that mechanism. Frequency,

policy's caution

\subsection{On-target counter transfer to AArch64}
\label{sec:eval-raspi4}
\input{tables/raspi4}

To validate the cross-ISA leg of the methodology from Sec.~\ref{sec:meth-crossisa} on the
deployment-class evaluation target, we ran the same frozen fixtures through the engine on a
Raspberry Pi~4 with a Cortex-A72 under the bare-metal \texttt{no\_std} kernel. An async task
parses the embedded fixture bytes, performs one counted align each, and asserts the counters
against the host values in Table~\ref{tab:raspi4}. Every counter of every fixture transfers
\emph{exactly}, or \raspiExact{} of \raspiTotal{}.

The first hardware run matched only 7 of 8 stored expectations. Bisection identified a
stale pre-fidelity-fix host snapshot, not an ISA effect; re-baselining reconciled host,
emulator, and hardware at \raspiDeviceOk{} of \raspiTotal{}. This incident demonstrates
the counter check's sensitivity and the need to commit-pin expected counters; the FMA, QEMU,
and subnormal checks are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:crossisa-debug}.

The timing columns of Table~\ref{tab:raspi4} report the \emph{Bare-metal} series, an amendment
to the measurement policy for OS-less targets. For each fixture, the series contains
\raspiTimingSamples{} warm and \raspiTimingCold{} cold samples on the warm map and is
bracketed by a fixed-work
calibration spin, the on-target analog of the host campaign's throttle guard and the
relevant hazard here, since the Pi~4 throttles under sustained load. Over the
${\sim}55$-minute run the calibration drifted at most \raspiCalibDriftPct\%: no thermal
throttling. The distributions are very tight: worst warm max/p50 across all fixtures is
${+}\raspiSpreadWorstPct\%$. The cold series uses an 8\,MiB eviction sweep before every
sample and sits at most ${+}\raspiColdDeltaWorstPct\%$ above the warm maximum, tighter than
the Isolated host series, as expected with no OS, one active core, and a
firmware-fixed clock. This platform provides conditions favorable to the factorization in
Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}: the work bound transfers exactly through the counters, and the
measured per-unit cost has sub-percent spread. These observations support deriving a
platform-specific unit-cost bound, but do not themselves establish one. In
absolute terms the serial configuration is not schedulable at 10\,Hz on this target:
\emph{search-00} costs \SI{\raspiSearchWarmMaxS}{s}, or $\raspiHostFactor\times$ the
x86 host's Isolated maximum. The non-shipped \emph{geom-stress} run costs
$\raspiLegalWorstBudgetX\times$ the \SI{100}{ms} budget, and Table~\ref{tab:raspi4}
separately reports \emph{shipped-osc}. These observations show that the A72 is inadequate
for the measured serial stress runs; they do not define a production-ECU speed requirement
or a shipped-input worst case. The x86 host, at
$\raspiHostFactorMin$--$\raspiHostFactorMax\times$ the A72 per unit work on these fixtures,
also misses the \emph{geom-stress} period by $\legalWorstBudgetXRust\times$, as shown in
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-twotier}. The affine $T_{\mathrm{obs,max}}(P)$ envelope of
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-psweep} lets any candidate platform be audited with a single on-target
$P$-sweep. Potential mechanisms for meeting the deadline include reducing $P$, applying
SIMD or algorithmic changes, and parallelizing the kernel. Section~\ref{sec:eval-parallel} measures approximately
\parRustSpeedupFour$\times$ at four host cores.

\subsection{Tail diagnostic and rejection of extrapolation}
\label{sec:eval-evt}
For each tail fixture we fitted per-session POT/GPD models to $3{\times}1000$ Isolated
samples and applied the full diagnostic gate. Lag-1--10 autocorrelation reaches
\evtAcfWorst{} rather than the ${\sim}0.03$ expected under independence; consequently the
bootstrap treats dependent exceedances as independent, invalidating its $q_{10^{-9}}$
intervals. We
therefore report no extrapolated exceedance probabilities and retain only the empirical
maxima in Table~\ref{tab:tails}. Table~\ref{tab:evt} in Appendix~\ref{app:evt} gives the
fixture-level diagnostics solely to document this rejection; plausible-looking fits do not
override failed assumptions.
