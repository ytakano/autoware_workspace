% Subnormal outlier: A/B-tested — NOT subnormals; per-point overhead on neighbor-sparse shape.
The \emph{subnormal} fixture's outlier ratio ($7.3\times$ at p50) demanded a controlled
check: re-generating the same fixture with the source shell pulled from \SI{1.99}{m} to
\SI{1.69}{m} --- moving every kernel $\exp$ result out of the subnormal band, all else equal
--- leaves the C++ time \emph{unchanged} (\SI{57.6}{ms} vs.\ \SI{58.6}{ms} p50; Rust 8.0
vs.\ 8.9). Subnormal operands are \emph{not} the mechanism on this microarchitecture (a
microbenchmark confirms subnormal-result \texttt{exp} costs only $2.9\times$/$5.1\times$ per
call for glibc/\texttt{libm}, negligible at this fixture's $\bar{K}=0.7$). The real driver is
the \emph{per-point constant overhead} exposed by a neighbor-sparse shape: with almost no
kernel work, C++ spends ${\approx}1.9$\,\si{\micro\second} per point per pass against
Rust's $0.26$ --- an ${\approx}1.6$\,\si{\micro\second} gap that reappears as the same
constant on \emph{cache-hostile} ($\bar{K}=3.0$). Decomposing it: the measured
allocator fast path (\SI{11.3}{ns} per malloc/free pair $\times$ ${\approx}10$ pairs) accounts
for only ${\sim}8\%$; the remainder is the per-query search machinery around
pcl/FLANN (call indirection and result-container handling) that the Rust engine's direct,
allocation-free kd query does not pay. This sharpens the allocation story of
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-alloc}: heap churn is cheap \emph{on average} --- its significance is the
unbounded latency tail --- while the mean-cost gap on sparse inputs is search-machinery
overhead. (No hardware performance counters are available in the measurement container, so the
final split is arithmetic over microbenchmarks rather than counter-attributed;
Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}.)

\subsection{Heap allocations per align}
\label{sec:eval-alloc}
% 11 mallocs/point/pass decomposition + the three layers. Rust proven 0.
The interposer counts decompose as ${\approx}11$ allocations per point per derivative pass on
every fixture (e.g.\ $\num{682248} / (31 \cdot 2000) = 11.0$), locating the source in the
per-point inner loop: a fresh neighbor vector constructed inside the parallel loop body, two
fresh index/distance vectors per radius-search call, and per-query result containers inside
pcl/FLANN (${\approx}8$/point). These ride the allocator's thread-cache fast path --- invisible
on average, but each is a potential lock, page fault, or fragmentation stall inside the
innermost loop. The Rust engine hoists all three layers into a pre-reserved workspace and
measures zero in the same harness, consistent with its allocation-freedom proof.

\subsection{Unit-cost regression}
\label{sec:eval-regression}
% T ~ a*nbr + b*kd + c per engine; R^2 >= 0.997. a: 133 vs 47 ns. Interpretation + caveat on
% the negative intercept (n=6 extrapolation artifact).
Regressing median latency on the (shared) counters yields $R^2 \ge \regRsqRust$ for both
engines: the two-term work model explains the timing. The per-kernel-evaluation cost is
\SI{\regKernelNsCpp}{ns} (C++) vs.\ \SI{\regKernelNsRust}{ns} (Rust) ---
$\regKernelRatio\times$ --- while C++'s per-kd-node cost is lower (\SI{\regKdNsCpp}{ns} vs.\
\SI{\regKdNsRust}{ns}); the kd term is second-order on the engine-level worst set, though it
dominates the deployment tier (Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-twotier}). (The small negative Rust
intercept is an extrapolation artifact of the small-$n$ fit.) The per-kernel-evaluation gap is
consistent with the same overhead structure as above --- each C++ evaluation reaches its leaf
through a \texttt{LeafConstPtr} indirection with the per-query machinery amortized over few
neighbors, where the Rust engine reads a flat leaf array --- though without hardware counters
we present this attribution as arithmetic-plausible rather than counter-proven.

\subsection{Two-tier bound: engine level vs.\ deployment level}
\label{sec:eval-twotier}
% The realizability question, answered by measurement. legal_worst (Sec 4) vs search_00.
The adversarial fixtures bound the engine at its API --- the right contract for the
\texttt{no\_std} kernel deployment, where inputs cross a trust boundary unvalidated. When the
production preprocessing contracts hold (Sec.~\ref{sec:impl-legal}), the reachable input space
shrinks, and with it the bound: on \emph{legal-worst}, the hardest preprocessing-reachable
input we can construct, the observed maximum drops to \SI{\legalWorstMaxRust}{ms} (Rust) /
\SI{\legalWorstMaxCpp}{ms} (C++) --- a factor of \legalWorstRatioRust$\times$ /
\legalWorstRatioCpp$\times$ below the engine-level union worst
(Table~\ref{tab:tails}). The mechanism is the $K$ ceiling: disjoint voxel-aligned map tiles cap
leaf multiplicity at 1, so $\bar{K} = \legalWorstKbar$ instead of 64, and the production
random-downsample cap fixes $P \le 1500$. Note the shift in the cost structure: kernel
evaluations drop $10.7\times$ ($\sumnbr$: $3.97\,\mathrm{M} \rightarrow 372\,\mathrm{k}$) but
time drops less, because kd-tree traversal --- unaffected by the $K$ ceiling --- becomes the
dominant term of the deployment tier. Read constructively, the pair quantifies what
\emph{enforcing input contracts at the component boundary} buys: validating tile disjointness
and scan-downsample invariants before the align tightens the defensible WCET bound by
$\legalWorstRatioRust\times$ without touching the kernel itself. The iteration axis of the
tier is not an artifact of the $\epsilon$ pin: the production-parameterized legal search
(Sec.~\ref{sec:impl-legal}) reaches the full 30-iteration cap on \emph{legal-osc}
(\SI{\legalOscMaxRust}{ms} Rust / \SI{\legalOscMaxCpp}{ms} C++, $\bar{K} = \legalOscKbar$) ---
below \emph{legal-worst}, which therefore remains the tier's time bound.

\subsection{Real-data replay: contract validation and degraded-prior behavior}
\label{sec:eval-realdata}
% Captured via the env-gated engine hook during an L1b run, replayed offline with counters.
To place the tiers against real inputs, we captured every NDT input (map tile, per-align guess
and source cloud) during a replay of a real urban drive (İstanbul; \realFrames{} aligns over
57 minutes through the production preprocessing) via an environment-gated hook at the engine's
entry points, then replayed the captured frames offline through both engines on identical
buffers with the cost counters enabled. Because the benchmark harness could not reproduce the
production EKF's prior quality offline, the guess track is a frozen NDT-odometry
reconstruction (constant-velocity extrapolation from a GNSS seed) --- \emph{representative of
degraded-prior operation} (initialization, EKF dropouts), not of nominal cruising, which the
converged node-level baseline shows at 3 iterations/frame.
Table~\ref{tab:realdata} shows the distributions; four observations. First, the preprocessing
contracts hold empirically: $P \le \realPMax = 1500$ on all \realFrames{} frames. Second, the
per-point neighbor count reaches at most $\realKmaxMax$: within the per-tile geometric bound
of 27, but \emph{one above} the voxel-center heuristic of 8 --- real centroids deviate from
voxel centers, pulling a ninth leaf into range; the load-bearing bound is the 27, not the 8.
Third, under degraded priors the iteration cap is \emph{routinely} reached on real geometry
(72\% of on-map frames), corroborating the deployment tier's iteration axis with real data;
the real worst frame costs \SI{\realCppMax}{ms} (C++) --- grazing the \SI{100}{ms} budget ---
vs.\ \SI{\realRustMax}{ms} (Rust), which sits $\realMarginDeploy\times$ below the
deployment-tier bound and ${\sim}\realMarginEngine\times$ below the engine tier. Fourth, equal
work holds on \realMatchPct\% of frames (\realMatch{} of \realFrames) --- and the initial
${\sim}2\%$ of exceptions became a product of the method itself: real rotated poses exposed
two port-fidelity gaps that every translation-only synthetic fixture is structurally blind to
(the C++ engine's initial-pass guess-matrix transform, and pcl's right-associated SSE transform
kernel). Fixing both reduced the divergence to the $0.12\%$ residual characterized in
Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}.

\subsection{Scaling with the input size $P$}
\label{sec:eval-psweep}
% Controlled experiment: P is the only variable (counters verify invariant per-point work).
% Affine model -> parametric bound -> budget inversion.
In the decomposition~\eqref{eq:decomp}, $P$ is the only multiplicative freedom: it scales the
per-iteration sum while leaving $N_{\mathrm{iter}}$, $K$, and $T_{\mathrm{solve}}$ untouched,
predicting an \emph{affine} law $\mathrm{WCET}(P) = \mathrm{slope}\cdot P + \mathrm{const}$. We
validate this with a controlled sweep: the union-worst geometry regenerated at $P \in
[\psweepPmin{}, \psweepPmax{}]$ (six points, $\times 2$ steps), where the counters certify that
per-point work is exactly invariant --- $N_{\mathrm{iter}} = 30$ and $\sumnbr = P \cdot 64
\cdot 31$ at every $P$ --- so time may vary only through $P$ itself.
Table~\ref{tab:psweep} and Fig.~\ref{fig:psweep} show the result: both engines are affine in
$P$ with $R^2 = \psweepRsqCpp{}$ (C++) and $\psweepRsqRust{}$ (Rust) on the \emph{max} series;
no cache knee is visible over the tested range. The fitted worst-case cost is
\SI{\psweepSlopeCpp}{\micro\second} (C++) vs.\ \SI{\psweepSlopeRust}{\micro\second} (Rust) per
source point, consistent with the per-kernel-evaluation costs of
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-regression} (${\approx}64$ kernel evaluations $\times$ 31 passes per point).

The affine law makes the bound \emph{parametric in the caller's contract}: inverting it against
the \SI{100}{ms} (10\,Hz) budget gives a maximum admissible downsampled-scan size of
$P \approx \psweepPmaxRust{}$ points for the Rust engine (\psweepPmaxCpp{} for C++) \emph{under
the union-worst geometry on this host} --- a number the localization node's voxel-filter
configuration can be audited against directly. (Host-relative; the target-hardware slope is the
number that matters for deployment. \todo{AArch64 slope, M5.})
