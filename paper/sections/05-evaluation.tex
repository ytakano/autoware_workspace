work, C++ spends ${\approx}\subnormalPtPassCppUs$\,\si{\micro\second} per point per pass against Rust's $\subnormalPtPassRustUs$
--- an ${\approx}\ptPassGapUs$\,\si{\micro\second} gap that reappears as the same constant on
\emph{cache-hostile} ($\bar{K}=\cacheHostileKbar$). Decomposing it: the measured allocator fast path
(\SI{\oneoffMallocPairNs}{ns} per malloc/free pair $\times$ ${\approx}\subnormalAllocPairs$ pairs) accounts for only
${\sim}\subnormalAllocSharePct\%$; the remainder is the per-query search machinery around pcl/FLANN (call
indirection and result-container handling) that the Rust engine's direct, allocation-free
kd query does not pay. This sharpens the allocation story of Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-alloc}: heap
churn is cheap \emph{on average} --- its significance is the unbounded latency tail --- while
the mean-cost gap on sparse inputs is search-machinery overhead. (No hardware performance
counters are available in the measurement container, so the final split is arithmetic over
microbenchmarks rather than counter-attributed; Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}.)

\subsection{Heap allocations per align}
\label{sec:eval-alloc}
The interposer counts decompose as ${\approx}\allocPerPtPass$ allocations per point per derivative pass on
every fixture (e.g.\ $\allocWorstPerAlign{} / (31 \cdot 2000) = \allocPerPtPassExact$), locating the source in the
per-point inner loop: a fresh neighbor vector constructed inside the parallel loop body, two
fresh index/distance vectors per radius-search call, and per-query result containers inside
pcl/FLANN (${\approx}8$/point). These ride the allocator's thread-cache fast path ---
invisible on average, but each is a potential lock, page fault, or fragmentation stall inside
the innermost loop. The Rust engine hoists all three allocation sites into a pre-reserved
workspace and measures zero in the same harness, consistent with its allocation-freedom proof
(Table~\ref{tab:alloc}).

\subsection{Unit-cost regression}
\label{sec:eval-regression}
Regressing median latency on the (shared) counters yields $R^2 \ge \regRsqRust$ for both
engines: the two-term work model explains the timing. The per-kernel-evaluation cost is
\SI{\regKernelNsCpp}{ns} (C++) vs.\ \SI{\regKernelNsRust}{ns} (Rust) ---
$\regKernelRatio\times$ --- while C++'s per-kd-node cost is lower (\SI{\regKdNsCpp}{ns} vs.\
\SI{\regKdNsRust}{ns}); the kd term is second-order on the engine-tier worst set, though it
dominates the deployment tier (Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-twotier}). (The small negative Rust
intercept is an extrapolation artifact of the small-$n$ fit.) The per-kernel-evaluation gap is
consistent with the same overhead structure as above --- each C++ evaluation reaches its leaf
through a pointer indirection with the per-query machinery amortized over few neighbors,
where the Rust engine reads a flat leaf array --- though without hardware counters we present
this attribution as arithmetic-plausible rather than counter-proven.

\subsection{Scaling with the input size $P$}
\label{sec:eval-psweep}
In the decomposition~\eqref{eq:decomp}, $P$ is the only multiplicative freedom: it scales the
per-iteration sum while leaving $N_{\mathrm{iter}}$, $K$, and $T_{\mathrm{solve}}$ untouched,
predicting an \emph{affine} law $\mathrm{WCET}(P) = \mathrm{slope}\cdot P + \mathrm{const}$.
We validate this with a controlled sweep: the union-worst geometry regenerated at $P \in
[\psweepPmin{}, \psweepPmax{}]$ (six points, $\times 2$ steps), where the counters certify
that per-point work is exactly invariant --- $N_{\mathrm{iter}} = 30$ and $\sumnbr = P \cdot
64 \cdot 31$ at every $P$ --- so time may vary only through $P$ itself.
Table~\ref{tab:psweep} and Fig.~\ref{fig:psweep} show the result: both engines are affine in
$P$ with $R^2 = \psweepRsqCpp{}$ (C++) and $\psweepRsqRust{}$ (Rust) on the \emph{max} series;
no cache knee is visible over the tested range. The fitted worst-case cost is
\SI{\psweepSlopeCpp}{\micro\second} (C++) vs.\ \SI{\psweepSlopeRust}{\micro\second} (Rust)
per source point, consistent with the per-kernel-evaluation costs of
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-regression} (${\approx}64$ kernel evaluations $\times$ 31 passes per
point).

The affine law makes the bound \emph{parametric in the caller's contract}: inverting it
against the \SI{100}{ms} (10\,Hz) budget gives a maximum admissible downsampled-scan size of
$P \approx \psweepPmaxRust{}$ points for the Rust engine (\psweepPmaxCpp{} for C++)
\emph{under the union-worst geometry on this host} --- a number the localization node's
voxel-filter configuration can be audited against directly. (Host-relative; on deployment hardware the same protocol
yields the slope that matters there.)

\subsection{Two-tier bound: engine tier vs.\ deployment tier}
\label{sec:eval-twotier}
The adversarial fixtures bound the engine at its API --- the right contract for the
\texttt{no\_std} kernel deployment, where inputs cross a trust boundary unvalidated. When the
production preprocessing contracts hold (Sec.~\ref{sec:impl-legal}), the reachable input
space shrinks, and with it the bound: on \emph{legal-worst}, the hardest
preprocessing-reachable input we can construct, the observed maximum drops to
\SI{\legalWorstMaxRust}{ms} (Rust) / \SI{\legalWorstMaxCpp}{ms} (C++) --- a factor of
\legalWorstRatioRust$\times$ / \legalWorstRatioCpp$\times$ below the engine-tier union worst
(Table~\ref{tab:tails}). The mechanism is the $K$ ceiling: disjoint voxel-aligned map tiles
cap leaf multiplicity at 1, so $\bar{K} = \legalWorstKbar$ instead of 64, and the production
random-downsample cap fixes $P \le 1500$. Note the shift in the cost structure: kernel
evaluations drop $\twoTierKernelDrop\times$ ($\sumnbr$: $\searchZeroSumNbrM\,\mathrm{M}
\rightarrow \legalWorstSumNbrK\,\mathrm{k}$)
but time drops less, because kd-tree traversal --- unaffected by the $K$ ceiling --- becomes
the dominant term of the deployment tier. Read constructively, the pair quantifies what
\emph{enforcing input contracts at the component boundary} buys: validating tile disjointness
and scan-downsample invariants before the align tightens the defensible WCET bound by
$\legalWorstRatioRust\times$ without touching the kernel itself. The iteration axis of the
tier is not an artifact of the $\epsilon$ pin: the production-parameterized legal search
(Sec.~\ref{sec:impl-legal}) reaches the full 30-iteration cap on \emph{legal-osc}
(\SI{\legalOscMaxRust}{ms} Rust / \SI{\legalOscMaxCpp}{ms} C++, $\bar{K} = \legalOscKbar$)
--- below \emph{legal-worst}, which therefore remains the tier's time bound.

\subsection{Real-data replay: contract validation and degraded-prior behavior}
\label{sec:eval-realdata}
To place the tiers against real inputs, we captured every NDT input (map tile, per-align
guess and source cloud) during a replay of a real urban drive (İstanbul; \realFrames{} aligns
over 57 minutes through the production preprocessing) via an environment-gated hook at the
engine's entry points, then replayed the captured frames offline through both engines on
identical buffers with the cost counters enabled. Because the benchmark harness could not
reproduce the production localization filter's prior quality offline, the guess track is a
frozen NDT-odometry reconstruction (constant-velocity extrapolation from a global-navigation-satellite-system (GNSS) seed) ---
\emph{representative of degraded-prior operation} (initialization, filter dropouts), not of
nominal cruising, which the converged node-level baseline shows at 3 iterations per frame.

One consequence of this open-loop track must be stated up front: the offline benchmark map is
a cropped extract, and the reconstructed trajectory exits it after frame \realOnMapSeqMax{} ---
so only \realOnMap{} aligns, a prefix window covering ${\sim}2\%$ of the drive, perform
meaningful matching work. We call a frame \emph{on-map} when at least one of its radius
searches returns a neighbor ($\sumnbr > 0$); on the remaining \realOffMap{} \emph{off-map}
frames every radius search returns empty and the align is a near-no-op (iteration count p50
$= \realOffMapIterMed$, C++ p50 ${\approx}\SI{\realOffMapCppMed}{ms}$). The per-frame
envelope rows of Table~\ref{tab:realdata} therefore use the on-map subset, while the $P$ and
max-$K$ contract checks --- which do not depend on map coverage --- use all frames.

Table~\ref{tab:realdata} shows the distributions; four observations. First, the preprocessing
contracts hold empirically: $P \le \realPMax = 1500$ on all \realFrames{} frames. Second, the
per-point neighbor count reaches at most $\realKmaxMax$: within the per-tile geometric bound
of 27, but \emph{one above} the voxel-center heuristic of 8 --- real centroids deviate from
voxel centers, pulling a ninth leaf into range; the load-bearing bound is the 27, not the 8.
Third, under degraded priors the iteration cap is \emph{routinely} reached on real geometry
(\realIterCapPct\% of on-map frames),
corroborating the deployment tier's iteration axis with real data; the real worst frame costs
\SI{\realCppMax}{ms} (C++) --- grazing the \SI{100}{ms} budget --- vs.\
\SI{\realRustMax}{ms} (Rust), which sits $\realMarginDeploy\times$ below the deployment-tier
bound and ${\sim}\realMarginEngine\times$ below the engine tier. Fourth, equal work holds on
\realMatch{} of \realFrames{} frames overall (\realMatchPct\%) --- but the honest denominator
is the on-map subset: all \realDivergences{} divergences fall on on-map frames, so the
equal-work rate over the frames doing meaningful matching work is \realOnMapMatchPct\%
(\realOnMapMatch{} of \realOnMap).
