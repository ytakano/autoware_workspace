\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Static WCET analyzers in the aiT tradition compute certified bounds from abstract cache and
pipeline
models~\cite{ferdinand2001ait}; Wilhelm et al.~\cite{wilhelm2008wcet} survey the field. These
methods excel on control-dominated code whose loop bounds are program constants or annotatable
invariants. In our kernel, the Newton iteration count, per-point neighbor multiplicity, and
kd-tree traversal length are properties of the \emph{input geometry}. Static analysis alone
can only return the product of the individual maxima in Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp}, with no
evidence that any input realizes it. Our first analysis layer retains the structural bound,
enforces its terms by construction, and checks them with property-based testing; the second
and third layers supply witness inputs and platform timing.

Measurement-based and probabilistic timing analysis (MBPTA) applies extreme value theory to
measured execution times to extrapolate
\pwcet{}~\cite{edgar2001gumbel,cucu2012mbpta}; Cazorla et al.~\cite{cazorla2019mbpta} give a
survey. A central limitation is \emph{representativity}: EVT bounds only the behaviors
exercised by the measurement protocol. This limitation has motivated randomized platforms
and measurement protocols~\cite{lima2017evt}, statistical estimation and validation
procedures~\cite{hansen2009statistical}, and reusable tooling~\cite{reghenzani2018chronovise}.
We address representativity from the input side by constructing stress inputs and evaluating
them with deterministic, timing-independent Rust counters. The domain-informed construction
reaches the Rust envelope's analytic maximum for the common kernel-evaluation term. A
secondary POT/GPD diagnostic on the frozen stress measurements
fails its independence gate, so we withhold tail extrapolation as detailed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval-evt}. The timing evidence therefore consists of observed maxima, not an
MBPTA estimate.

Search-based generation of worst-case inputs for timing constraints dates to Wegener et
al.~\cite{wegener1997testing,wegener2001comparison}, who evolve test data with \emph{measured
execution time} as fitness and note the complementarity with static analysis. Wall-clock
fitness is noisy, machine-specific, and unreproducible across platforms. Our fitness
combines a common kernel-work counter with a Rust-specific traversal counter. For a fixed
port implementation, the resulting input ranking is deterministic and independent of timing
noise. Analytic saturation is established by checking the common counter against its bound,
not by a comparative advantage of the counter fitness. The common kernel-work axis supports
cross-language transfer: a Rust-search input is replayed
on the production C++ engine, and the replay checks work-trace conformance per input. The
Rust traversal counter does not transfer; C++ results are observations over those transferred
inputs and do not establish a C++-specific traversal extremum.

For ROS-based systems, Casini et al.~\cite{casini2019ros2} bound end-to-end response times
of ROS~2 processing chains under reservation-based scheduling, treating per-callback WCETs as given inputs. Such analyses
are complementary to ours: we establish a structural work envelope and timing evidence for
the dominant callback kernel, the \ndt{} align, that those chain analyses consume. We also
remove per-point heap allocation and other structural
hazards that would otherwise make the per-callback WCET assumption fragile.

In real-time and safety-critical systems, Rust's ownership model underpins systems such as
the Tock embedded
OS~\cite{levy2017tock}, and RustBelt~\cite{jung2018rustbelt} gives machine-checked foundations
for its safety claims. In safe Rust, ownership, lifetime, and aliasing rules discharge
memory-safety and data-race-freedom obligations that an equivalent C++ implementation would
need to establish through its own design and verification evidence. This advantage does not
establish WCET: panic-freedom, allocation-freedom, recursion-freedom, and bounded loops on
the hot path remain separate requirements. We show these can be made lint- and test-enforced
properties of a production kernel. In this case study, the high-fidelity,
per-input-checked port also serves as an analysis instrument: its safe core narrows the
verification surface while carrying the instrumentation burden for both languages.

For \ndt{} scan matching, registration originates with Biber and
Stra{\ss}er~\cite{biber2003ndt} and was extended to 3D by
Magnusson~\cite{magnusson2009thesis}. The engine studied here is Autoware's
production localization matcher~\cite{autoware}, a multigrid variant of the multi-threaded
\texttt{ndt\_omp} implementation~\cite{ndtomp}. Prior work on these implementations reports
average-case throughput. We are not aware of a published WCET-oriented analysis of an \ndt{} engine.
